Two things in it changed how I schedule a week.

The first is the work/leisure paradox. People report flow far more often at work than in their free time, and still say they would rather be somewhere else. Whatever we think we are protecting when we protect leisure, it is mostly not the good part of the day.

The second is attention as the only currency. He wrote that before smartphones and it reads now like someone describing a thing he could not have seen. Everything you will ever experience has to be bought with attention, and the supply per day is fixed. That is the whole argument for the phone leaving the room, and it is stronger than any productivity framing of the same rule.

Where it stops: flow is morally empty. A surgeon, a burglar and someone refreshing a dashboard are all in it, and the state is identical from the inside. He gives this a paragraph and moves on. It is a much bigger hole than that — if the state can't distinguish a vocation from a compulsion, it can't be the criterion for a good life on its own. It needs something above it to say what is worth entering flow *for*.

The other crack is quieter: the prescription is always to change your relationship to the conditions, never the conditions. Useful if you are the one who sets them. Convenient if you are not.

Complete by chapter five. Chapters six to nine apply the same idea to one domain after another and you can skim them without losing anything.
