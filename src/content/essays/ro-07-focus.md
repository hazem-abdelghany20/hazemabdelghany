The first skill is holding the blade steady, and I need to clear away the biggest lie about it first: focus is not a state you find. It is a skill you build. People wait for focus the way they wait for weather — the right morning, the right mood, the right café — and then wonder why the <button class="ref" data-ref="deep-work">deep work</button> never arrives. Nobody waits to feel like squatting heavy. You train, and the capacity grows. Focus is exactly that kind of thing, and the evidence is that yours used to be better. You read whole books as a kid. Something trained the capacity *down*.

That something has a mechanism, so let me name it. Every time you sit at the edge of something hard and reach for the phone at the first flicker of discomfort, you complete a training rep — just not the one you wanted. You are teaching the mind, with perfect consistency, that difficulty is a cue for escape. Ten thousand reps later the response is automatic: hard thing, twitch, reach. Nothing is wrong with your brain. Your brain has learned exactly what you drilled into it. The distracted mind is not broken. It is *superbly trained in the wrong direction* — which, if trainability is real, means it can be trained back.

The counter-drill has three parts, and none of them is clever.

**One thing at a time, on a clock.** The research on task-switching is unambiguous: every glance away leaves a residue — part of your attention stays with the thing you glanced at, for many minutes after. A "quick check" is never thirty seconds; it is thirty seconds plus a twenty-minute tax on the depth of everything that follows. Six checks in an afternoon and you were never deep at all — you spent four hours in the shallow end and called it a workday, wondering why you're exhausted with nothing to show. So: one task, one block, a visible timer, nothing else. The timer matters more than it should. It converts focus from a mood into a rep — started, held, finished, counted.

**Make the escape expensive.** Willpower is a terrible security system; it guards the door for an hour and then falls asleep. Distance guards it all day. The phone goes in another room — not face down, not in the pocket, *another room* — because the twitch will come, and when it comes, a twenty-step walk is enough friction for the forebrain to catch up and veto. This is my oldest standing rule, the one I rebuild first whenever my system collapses, because every other rule leans on it. The knight did not test his discipline by sleeping in the enemy camp. He put a wall between himself and the thing that wanted him.

**Train on boredom itself.** This is the rep nobody does. The reaching habit isn't built during work; it's built in the gaps — the queue, the elevator, the red light, every micro-boredom instantly medicated with the screen. Those gaps are your training yard. Stand in the queue with your hands empty. Let the light be red. It costs nothing, nobody notices, and it is the exact muscle: staying present to a moment that offers no stimulation. If you cannot survive a red light without the phone, the two-hour block was never going to happen.

Build toward blocks the way you'd load a bar: twenty-five clean minutes before you attempt ninety. And know what you're building toward, because the compounding is real: focus deepens within a session — minute forty reaches places minute five cannot — and across months, the practice rebuilds your baseline until concentration stops feeling like war. Then the flow conditions of Chapter 6 stop being a lucky visit and start being a place you know the road to.

The blade was always sharp. The skill is your grip.
