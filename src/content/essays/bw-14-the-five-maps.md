<section>
<p>These five between them account for most of what is taught about building wealth. They are presented here the way a surveyor presents competing maps of the same ground: what each one claims, what it gets right, and where it will walk you off a cliff. None of them is useless. None of them is sufficient. And every one was written by someone whose own fortune came from a slightly different mechanism than the one they are selling.</p>
<!-- FW1 -->
<div class="fw">
<div class="fw-head"><h3>Specific knowledge and leverage</h3><span class="src">Naval Ravikant</span></div>
<p class="claim">Wealth comes from applying knowledge society cannot train you for, under your own name, through assets that replicate at zero marginal cost — and because that last kind of leverage needs nobody's permission, a person with a laptop can now reach it.</p>
<div class="fw-grid">
<div class="fw-box"><span class="lbl">Mechanics</span><ul>
<li><strong>Wealth ≠ money ≠ status.</strong> Wealth is assets that earn while you sleep. Money is a transferable claim on other people's time. Status is zero-sum and is the reason people sabotage each other.</li>
<li><strong>The equation is conjunctive:</strong> specific knowledge × accountability × leverage. Not additive — a zero in any term zeroes the product.</li>
<li><strong>Specific knowledge</strong> is what you cannot be trained for. If society can train you, it can train your replacement.</li>
<li><strong>Accountability</strong> means taking risk under your own name, so that reputation accrues to you rather than to an employer.</li>
<li><strong>Four leverages split by permission:</strong> labour and capital need someone's consent; code and media do not.</li>
</ul></div>
<div class="fw-box test"><span class="lbl">The test, this week</span><p>Write down what you currently get paid for. Then ask: if you stopped tomorrow, how long until someone with your job title could be trained to replace you? If the answer is under six months, you have no specific knowledge yet — you have a role. Then ask the second question: of your last twelve months of income, how much arrived as a fee for hours, and how much as a share of something's upside? If the second number is zero, the leverage question is premature.</p></div>
<div class="fw-box wrong"><span class="lbl">Where it is wrong</span><ul>
<li><strong>His own fortune contradicts it.</strong> Trace the money and it is capital and position — equity bought with cash, fund carry, and founder stock in a company that required a regulator's explicit blessing. Code wrote none of it, and media he deliberately declined to monetise.</li>
<li><strong>Multipliers do nothing to zero.</strong> All four leverages applied to someone with no specific knowledge, no network and no capital return nothing. The honest order is specific knowledge <em>first</em> — five to ten unglamorous years that feel like falling behind — and the framework badly underplays that.</li>
<li><strong>"Permissionless" is doing concealed work.</strong> It is true that nobody must approve your code. It is also true that deal flow, distribution and trust are entirely permissioned, and he had all three before he started.</li>
</ul></div>
</div>
</div>

<div class="fw">
<div class="fw-head"><h3>The CENTS filter and the three roads</h3><span class="src">MJ DeMarco</span></div>
<p class="claim">Wealth comes from owning a business that passes five gates simultaneously — you Control it, it sits behind real barriers to Entry, it serves a paying Need, its income is detached from your Time, and it Scales beyond geography — because such a business produces both profit and a sellable asset.</p>
<div class="fw-grid">
<div class="fw-box"><span class="lbl">Mechanics</span><ul>
<li><strong>Three roads, sorted by their wealth equation.</strong> Sidewalk: wealth = income + debt. Slowlane: wealth = job + market returns, which works but takes forty years. Fastlane: wealth = net profit × a multiple, because a business can be sold.</li>
<li><strong>CENTS is a pass/fail filter run before you build,</strong> not a scorecard afterwards. Most first ideas fail Entry and Time.</li>
<li><strong>The asset-value multiplier is the actual engine.</strong> A business netting $100,000 a year is not worth $100,000 — in an industry trading at a multiple it is worth several times that, and the multiple is where the wealth lives.</li>
<li><strong>Producer, not consumer.</strong> Sell shovels rather than dig for gold.</li>
</ul></div>
<div class="fw-box test"><span class="lbl">The test, this week</span><p><strong>The sellability test.</strong> Ninety minutes, five numbers, on whatever actually pays you right now. (1) Your last three months' net profit from it. (2) Name one specific real company or person — by name, not a category — who would pay cash for it and then own it <em>without you in it</em>. (3) What breaks in the first month after you leave. (4) How many customers it could serve before you personally became the constraint. (5) The price at which you would genuinely sell. If you cannot name a buyer at step two, you do not own an asset. You own a job with better branding.</p></div>
<div class="fw-box wrong"><span class="lbl">Where it is wrong</span><ul>
<li><strong>It is survivorship bias formalised into law.</strong> CENTS was reverse-engineered from exactly one outcome — a web directory that won a category in a pre-Google window — and then presented as universal. Nobody has ever run it across a cohort of failures to see whether it separates them.</li>
<li><strong>The originating win was a timing arbitrage coded as skill.</strong> An exact-match domain bought for around $10,000 could own an entire category in a world before search engines and review sites. That is an era, not a method.</li>
<li><strong>Applied strictly, CENTS rejects most people who actually got rich.</strong> Law firms, consultancies and medical practices fail Time and Scale. Hormozi's business started as six gyms, which fails almost every gate.</li>
<li><strong>Its definition of risk is inverted for anyone without a safety net.</strong> "Might not get rich" and "might not make rent" are not the same class of risk. Ruin is absorbing — you cannot iterate after it.</li>
</ul></div>
</div>
</div>

<div class="fw">
<div class="fw-head"><h3>Moats, circle of competence, capital allocation</h3><span class="src">Buffett &amp; Munger · Thorndike</span></div>
<p class="claim">Durable wealth comes from owning businesses that earn high returns on the capital actually tied up in them, protected by a barrier competitors cannot cross, bought only inside the narrow set of businesses you can genuinely evaluate — then compounded by routing every dollar they throw off to its highest available return.</p>
<div class="fw-grid">
<div class="fw-box"><span class="lbl">Mechanics</span><ul>
<li><strong>The moat is a structural barrier, not a good product.</strong> A great business needs an enduring moat protecting excellent returns on invested capital.</li>
<li><strong>Return on <em>incremental</em> invested capital</strong> is the real test, and the one most people skip. Not "is this profitable" but "what does the next dollar I put in earn?"</li>
<li><strong>Owner earnings</strong> replaces reported profit: cash generated, minus what you must spend simply to stay the same size.</li>
<li><strong>Circle of competence is a boundary, not a size.</strong> How big it is matters far less than knowing where its edge is.</li>
<li><strong>Capital allocation is the real job</strong> — most operators are promoted for operating skill and have never allocated anything.</li>
</ul></div>
<div class="fw-box test"><span class="lbl">The test, this week</span><p><strong>The reinvestment test.</strong> On whatever already earns you money. Write last year's cash in minus cash out. Then subtract what you had to spend just to stay the same size — the replaced laptop, the restocked inventory, the ad spend that merely held volume flat. What remains is owner earnings. Now the decisive question: <em>the last $1,000 you put into this, what did it return?</em> Not the average across the business — the marginal dollar. Then act on that number rather than the average, which is what almost nobody does.</p></div>
<div class="fw-box wrong"><span class="lbl">Where it is wrong</span><ul>
<li><strong>The flagship stopped outperforming around the time the framework became famous.</strong> Split the record at the point the letters became canon and the second half looks far more ordinary than the first.</li>
<li><strong>The alpha was substantially leverage and factor exposure</strong> — cheap insurance float applied to high-quality low-volatility businesses — not the qualitative judgement the books teach.</li>
<li><strong>Circle of competence is unfalsifiable after the fact,</strong> which makes it useless as a decision rule and excellent as an excuse. Every win is retroactively inside the circle; every loss retroactively outside it.</li>
<li><strong>It presupposes the capital it claims to create.</strong> "Allocate capital well" is superb advice for someone who already commands retained earnings and meaningless for someone at zero.</li>
</ul></div>
</div>

<div class="fw">
<div class="fw-head"><h3>Monopoly over competition</h3><span class="src">Peter Thiel</span></div>
<p class="claim">Competition destroys profit, so the only rational goal of a new business is to escape it entirely — and the reliable route is to take 100% of a market small enough to actually dominate, then expand outward from that base.</p>
<div class="fw-grid">
<div class="fw-box"><span class="lbl">Mechanics</span><ul>
<li><strong>Value created versus value captured.</strong> A business can generate enormous value for the world and keep almost none of it — airlines being the archetype.</li>
<li><strong>The market-definition lie detector, pointed both ways.</strong> Losing firms gerrymander their market to sound dominant; winning firms understate theirs to avoid scrutiny.</li>
<li><strong>Start small and monopolise.</strong> Reach a few thousand people who genuinely need the thing and take a dominant share, rather than chasing 1% of a huge market.</li>
<li><strong>Secrets.</strong> The zone between accepted convention and genuine mystery — things that are true and important but that most people do not yet believe.</li>
</ul></div>
<div class="fw-box test"><span class="lbl">The test, this week</span><p><strong>The denominator test.</strong> Write your market in one sentence <em>the way a customer would say it</em>, not the way you would pitch it. Estimate the total money spent in that market, in your city or country, last month. Divide your revenue by it. If the number is under 1%, you are not differentiated — you are one of many, and your pricing will prove it. Then write your secret as a single testable sentence: "Most people in this market believe X; I know Y." Spend the week having ten conversations with buyers. If you cannot find three who will pay <em>because</em> Y is true, you do not have a secret. You have an opinion.</p></div>
<div class="fw-box wrong"><span class="lbl">Where it is wrong</span><ul>
<li><strong>The four characteristics of monopoly are outputs, not inputs.</strong> He looked at monopolies and listed what monopolies have. Network effects, economies of scale and brand are consequences of already having won. You cannot select them on day one.</li>
<li><strong>His own flagship company is a counterexample.</strong> PayPal did not escape competition — it survived a knife-fight, merging with its largest rival and fighting several incumbents at once.</li>
<li><strong>The fortune did not come from the framework.</strong> His transformative win was a passive half-million-dollar cheque into Facebook, introduced through his network. That is access, not monopoly theory.</li>
<li><strong>"Definite optimism," applied with his own money, detonated.</strong> A single high-conviction macro thesis held without feedback loops — exactly the posture the book prescribes over iteration — and the fund collapsed.</li>
<li><strong>Ignore the unprofitable-for-years clause entirely.</strong> That is available to people with his balance sheet. Set a hard rule instead: cash-positive within six months, or the idea is wrong.</li>
</ul></div>

<div class="fw-head"><h3>Survival first</h3><span class="src">Housel · Taleb · the survivorship critique</span></div>
<p class="claim">Over a long enough horizon, differences in outcome are driven more by who avoided ruin and stayed in the game than by who picked better — and because the entire advice corpus is sampled from survivors, it systematically under-reports the failure rate of the exact behaviour it recommends.</p>
<div class="fw-grid">
<div class="fw-box"><span class="lbl">Mechanics</span><ul>
<li><strong>The average outcome is not your outcome.</strong> A bet with positive expected value across many people can still ruin almost every individual who takes it repeatedly, because your losses compound on your own capital, not on the average's.</li>
<li><strong>Absorbing barriers dominate expected value.</strong> Any strategy with a non-zero chance of total loss per period reaches zero eventually, given enough periods. Position sizing beats being right.</li>
<li><strong>Duration beats rate.</strong> Compounding is exponent-driven, so years in the game matter more than percentage points of return.</li>
<li><strong>Volatility is a fee, not a fine.</strong> Drawdowns are the price of admission. The failure mode is not picking wrong — it is exiting during the fee.</li>
<li><strong>Room for error.</strong> Deliberately hold a position that is suboptimal against your forecast, so the range you survive is wider than the range you predicted.</li>
</ul></div>
<div class="fw-box test"><span class="lbl">The test, this week</span><p><strong>Build your ruin column.</strong> One page, thirty minutes, real numbers. List every exposure that could take you to zero in the next twelve months, with your honest probability next to each: your largest client as a percentage of revenue; your largest fixed payment against your worst month of the last year; the currency you save in versus the currency you spend; any personal guarantee you have signed; any single platform, account or relationship whose loss ends your income. <em>The row with the highest product of probability and severity is the only thing you should work on this month.</em> Not the growth idea. That one.</p></div>
<div class="fw-box wrong"><span class="lbl">Where it is wrong</span><ul>
<li><strong>It commits the survivorship error it names.</strong> Its evidence is Buffett, Gates and a handful of quiet millionaires — every one selected because they ended rich, with the causal behaviour inferred backwards.</li>
<li><strong>As advice it is unfalsifiable.</strong> "Be patient," "leave room for error," "be reasonable" — no quantity is ever specified, so no outcome could contradict it.</li>
<li><strong>The barbell presupposes the wealth it is meant to build.</strong> A large very-safe allocation plus a small speculative one requires a large safe allocation to exist. Someone at zero has no safe 90%.</li>
<li><strong>"Avoid ruin" is itself a slow absorbing barrier.</strong> In a 20–30% inflation economy, never risking anything is not survival — it is guaranteed decay. The framework has no theory of when to take the real risk.</li>
<li><strong>Skin in the game makes people sincere, not correct.</strong> Having everything on the line is fully compatible with being confidently wrong — which is precisely what the base rate of business failure consists of.</li>
</ul></div>
