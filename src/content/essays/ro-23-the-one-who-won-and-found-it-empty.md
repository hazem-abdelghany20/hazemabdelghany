This knight the stories love best, right up until the last page they never write. He took the hill. All of it — the campaign prevailed, the name made, the halls hung with proof. And in the quiet after the feast, in the room every trophy was supposed to fill, he made the discovery that has ambushed winners in every century: *the arrival was empty.* Not disappointing — empty. The scoreboard filled and nothing happened inside. And because he had mortgaged twenty years to the belief that this exact moment would pay everything back, the emptiness didn't read as a mood. It read as a verdict on the twenty years — the most expensive silence a man can hear.

Psychology has a modest name for the mild version — arrival fallacy, the well-documented gap between the happiness we project onto a future win and the flat weather that actually attends it. But the mechanics deserve better than a label, and you own all the parts by now. **The horse can only want; it cannot have.** Chapter 3's machinery is an anticipation engine — dopamine runs on *maybe*, on the gap between here and there, and at the summit the gap is gone, so the engine simply falls silent; the silence is not a malfunction, it is the spec sheet. The wanting was never *for* the having; biologically, the wanting was for the chase. **And the chase, meanwhile, had been paying the whole time** — this is the cruelest part, and the most redemptive, depending which way you turn it. <button class="ref" data-ref="csikszentmihalyi">Csikszentmihalyi</button>'s finding from Chapter 6, now at the scale of a life: when people describe the best years they ever lived, they describe *campaigns* — stakes real, attention gathered, ability barely sufficient, company mounted alongside. The climb was dense with exactly the alive-ness the summit was supposed to deliver. The winner's despair is a bookkeeping error: he billed twenty years of payment to a moment that was never the paying kind, then read the moment's silence as proof he was never paid.

So the diagnosis is almost gentle: nothing is wrong with him, and — hear this precisely — nothing was wrong with the winning either. The error was the *theology* of it: he made the summit his ledger of record, asked an output to do what outputs cannot do — confer meaning — and Chapter 19 already showed you meaning never lived there. It lives on the input side: in the stance, the work, the love, the intention. He had meaning in abundance, for twenty years, *while climbing* — he just believed the whole time that it was stored at the top, and so arrived at the vault to find it was never the vault; his wealth had been paid out daily, in wages he'd been spending without noticing.

What does he do, standing in the feast-hall silence? Three true things. **Grieve the theology, not the years** — the years were real and dense and paid in full; what died at the summit was only a false accounting, and mourning it takes less time than he fears. **Learn shukr in the flatness** — gratitude, in the tradition, is not a feeling that arrives with the win; it is a *practice* aimed at what is already in hand, and it is the only reliable answer to the horse's silence, because it is the one form of attention that makes the *having* vivid instead of the wanting. The summit is genuinely good — the view, the security, the proof of craft — but only gratitude can make goodness felt; the horse never could. **And then, in time, choose a next hill — for the right reason now.** Not to outrun the emptiness (that's the horse's pitch, and it leads to serial summiting, each peak flatter than the last — the winner's version of tolerance, Chapter 3 at the scale of decades), but because he now knows *where the living actually happens*, and wants to be where it happens, with his eyes finally open. The blessed campaign is the one begun by a man who no longer needs its summit.

The knight who won, grieved the false vault, gave thanks, and rode out again lighter — he is not restarting. He is, for the first time, playing the game where it was always being played. Some men need the summit to learn this. If this book spares you the twenty-year tuition, it has earned its price.
