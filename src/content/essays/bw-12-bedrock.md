<section>
<p>Here is the method, because the method is the only thing separating this section from every other book that tells you what rich people have in common.</p>
<p>Nine statements were drafted from the ten cases — the sort of thing that normally appears in a list like this. Each was then handed to a hostile reader whose explicit instruction was to <em>refute</em> it: find the case where it is false, or show that it is true only because of how the ten were selected, or show that it is stretchy enough to survive any evidence at all.</p>
<p>That last failure mode is the dangerous one. A claim that cannot be wrong feels profound and teaches nothing. "Every wealthy person had a floor underneath them" sounds like insight, until you notice the floor was defined as <em>a family, a salary, savings, a credential, or a country</em> — a list so wide that only a stateless orphan fails it. It scored ten out of ten and it is worthless, and that combination is exactly what makes it worth naming.</p>
<div class="note warn">
<span class="lbl">What did not survive</span>
<p><strong>"It always takes decades."</strong> False. Bezos reached a billion in four years. Rihanna's beauty company did it in four. Hormozi went from $1,036 and six figures of debt to roughly a hundred million in five.</p>
<p><strong>"They all went through a long public humiliating period."</strong> False. Blakely was never broke — she kept her day job, took no investment, carried no debt, and the company was profitable in year one. Zell's famous humiliation came <em>ten months after</em> he banked $39 billion.</p>
<p><strong>"They kept their living costs separate from the compounding asset."</strong> False for at least three. Bezos has sold roughly $48 billion of Amazon stock since 2002 and was selling from 1998. Arnault's family holding collects around a billion euros a year in dividends. Hormozi sold all six gyms to fund the next thing.</p>
<p><strong>"Each was world-class at exactly one leverage."</strong> False for eight of the ten, and the correction is the most useful finding in this book. It appears below as the fifth invariant.</p>
<p><strong>"Each chose a share of the upside over a guaranteed fee."</strong> Inverted, in Hormozi's case. His decisive move was to <em>stop</em> owning gyms and start charging gym owners a fee.</p>
</div>
<h3>The eight that held</h3>
<p>Each of these survived a genuine attempt to kill it, and each is stated in the narrowest form that still holds. They are less inspiring than the versions that died. That is generally a good sign.</p>
<div class="verdicts">
<div class="verdict">
<div class="v-top"><span class="v-claim">01 · The runway</span><span class="stamp u">10 / 10</span></div>
<p><strong>Not one of them built the money engine first.</strong> Every single one spent between four and eighteen years being paid by someone else, learning the trade, or building an audience, <em>before the vehicle that made the money existed.</em></p>
<p class="ex">Buffett: Columbia and working for Graham before the 1956 partnership. Bezos: eight years across three firms to senior vice-president. Arnault: thirteen years inside the family company. Huang: ten years at AMD and LSI Logic. Rihanna: twelve years of music before the equity deal. Hormozi: two years of consulting salary, then three years of running gyms badly. Blakely: seven years selling fax machines door to door.</p>
<p>This replaces "it takes decades," and it is far more useful because it tells you what the time is <em>for</em>. The duration of the engine varies enormously — four years to thirty-six. The runway does not vary at all. <strong>There are zero beginners in this book who found the right idea.</strong></p>
</div>
<div class="verdict">
<div class="v-top"><span class="v-claim">02 · Nobody could force them to sell</span><span class="stamp u">10 / 10</span></div>
<p><strong>In every case, before the bad period arrived, they had arranged things so that no lender, investor or creditor had the power to make them liquidate at the bottom.</strong></p>
<p class="ex">Buffett: permanent capital and insurance float, with no redemption rights. Bezos: founder control, plus a convertible bond raised weeks before the window shut in 2000. Zell's personal rule was literally "thou shalt not sign" — non-recourse debt, no personal guarantees. Blakely: no investors and no debt, so there was nobody to answer to. Huang: a board that declined to fire him through an 84% drawdown.</p>
<p>This is what the humiliation story was pointing at and getting wrong. Enduring a bad period is not the qualification — humiliation is the most abundant input in business, and most people who endure it stay broke. <strong>Being <em>unforceable</em> when the bad period arrives is the qualification</strong>, and it is a structural decision made years earlier, in calm weather, about capital structure.</p>
<p>The control case sits inside the sample. Tribune is the one deal where creditors owned the timing rather than Zell, and it went to zero.</p>
</div>
<div class="verdict">
<div class="v-top"><span class="v-claim">03 · They controlled the conditions, never the event</span><span class="stamp u">10 / 10</span></div>
<p><strong>None of them controlled the event that paid them. All of them controlled whether they were still solvent, still in position, and still holding the asset when it arrived.</strong></p>
<p class="ex">Buffett did not control when a seller got annoyed enough to sell him an insurance company, or when 2008 panicked — he controlled staying liquid for sixty years. Huang did not commission three researchers in Toronto to train a neural network on his gaming cards — he controlled being thirty days from payroll and surviving it. Blakely did not cause Oprah — she controlled being in Neiman Marcus, Saks and QVC first, so that when Oprah happened there was something to buy.</p>
<p>This is the honest version of the luck argument, and it splits into four things you can actually work on: <strong>solvency</strong> (do not die before the event), <strong>duration</strong> (be present for more years, so more events can find you), <strong>ownership</strong> (hold a claim, so an event that arrives pays <em>you</em>), and <strong>exposure</strong> (be somewhere uncontrolled upside can physically reach you — publish, ship, be visible, be reachable).</p>
</div>
<div class="verdict">
<div class="v-top"><span class="v-claim">04 · Two leverages — one theirs, one borrowed</span><span class="stamp u">10 / 10</span></div>
<p><strong>Every one of these fortunes required at least two forms of leverage. Only one was the person's own distinctive skill. The second was borrowed, inherited, bought, granted by a regulator, or lucked into.</strong></p>
<p class="ex">Buffett: his own was capital allocation; the second was float, <em>bought</em> by purchasing insurers. Bezos: his own was long-horizon system design; the second was roughly $2bn of bond-market money and a cheque from his parents. Arnault: his own was manufactured scarcity; the second was a Lazard syndicate and French state money. Sawiris: his own was underwriting risk nobody else would price; the second was a licence <em>granted by a government</em>.</p>
<p>This replaces the appealing and false "pick one leverage" — following that rule strictly would have killed eight of these ten fortunes. <strong>The true instruction comes in two parts: become world-class at one thing, then go and acquire the second thing you will never be world-class at.</strong></p>
<p>And there is a hard corollary about which one you get to choose. Of the six leverages, only <strong>code</strong> and <strong>media</strong> can be practised daily without anyone's permission. Capital, debt, position and brand-at-scale all require somebody's yes — a bank, a regulator, a retailer, an audience that already exists — and <em>every person here who led with one of those four already had the yes before they started.</em></p>
</div>
<div class="verdict">
<div class="v-top"><span class="v-claim">05 · Not one of them invented anything</span><span class="stamp u">10 / 10</span></div>
<p>Spanx is cut-off pantyhose. Amazon is a store. LVMH is other people's century-old names. Berkshire is other people's companies. Fenty Beauty is manufactured by someone else's laboratory. Hormozi sold instructions to a turnaround he had already run thirty-three times. Sawiris bought licences other operators had refused. Nvidia is the only arguable case, and even there the innovation was a software ecosystem wrapped around silicon in a market that already had a dozen entrants.</p>
<p><strong>The shared engine is distribution and pricing of an existing thing, not novelty.</strong> This is worth dwelling on, because "I do not have an original idea" is one of the most common reasons capable people never start, and it is disqualifying in none of these ten cases.</p>
</div>
<div class="verdict">
<div class="v-top"><span class="v-claim">06 · A boring cash business paid for the interesting one</span><span class="stamp u">10 / 10</span></div>
<p><strong>In every case the glamorous, compounding asset was cross-subsidised by an unglamorous operating engine that nobody writes chapters about.</strong></p>
<p class="ex">Nvidia's gaming margin paid for a decade of CUDA. Berkshire's insurance underwriting paid for the equities. Amazon's supplier terms and customer cash paid for two decades of suppressed margin. Rihanna's touring and endorsement <em>fees</em> paid for the twelve years before Fenty existed. Hormozi's gym cash funded Gym Launch. Blakely's fax-machine salary funded the first two years. Buffett's performance fee on other people's money funded the first thirteen.</p>
<p>Notice how far this goes beyond "keep a day job." The subsidy is not incidental — in several cases the boring engine <em>was</em> the business, and the ownership was assembled out of its cash. <strong>The unglamorous thing paying for the glamorous thing is not a compromise. It is the structure.</strong></p>
</div>
<div class="verdict">
<div class="v-top"><span class="v-claim">07 · The next unit always cost less than the last</span><span class="stamp u">10 / 10</span></div>
<p><strong>Not one of them sold something with a linear labour cost.</strong> Insurance float. A marketplace. Brand pricing power on a manufactured good. Leverage on real assets. A seed portfolio. Prepaid network minutes. Silicon plus a software moat. Information products at $6,000 with no delivery cost. A $20 garment costing a few dollars to make.</p>
<p>Hormozi's own pivot is the cleanest statement of the rule: he stopped operating gyms, which are labour-bound, and started selling the instructions, which are not. <em>In all ten engines, the next unit is cheaper than the one before it.</em> If yours is not, you have bought yourself a job — possibly a very good one, but not this.</p>
</div>
<div class="verdict">
<div class="v-top"><span class="v-claim">08 · An outside institution had to put a price on it</span><span class="stamp n">9 / 10</span></div>
<p><strong>The fortune became real only when a large, well-capitalised third party was both willing and able to price the claim.</strong> An IPO, a trade sale, or a strategic partner's carrying value.</p>
<p class="ex">Blakely was not a billionaire until Blackstone priced Spanx in October 2021 — twenty-three years in. Huang's 3.58% is worth whatever the market says this morning. Rihanna's billion is a partner's mark on a joint venture, currently falling, none of it converted to cash. Naval is the exception and the proof: an illiquid private mark and a sealed settlement give him the softest number in the set, precisely because nobody large has priced it.</p>
<p>So the instruction is not "own an appreciating asset" — it is <strong>own a claim somebody credible can eventually price.</strong> An asset only you believe in is a hobby with paperwork.</p>
</div>
</div>
<div class="note ochre">
<span class="lbl">The one that nearly held — and the exception is instructive</span>
<p>A ninth claim came very close: <em>the payment for the work that made them rich was never a fee — it was a claim settled after the fact by an outcome they did not fully control, one that could have paid zero.</em> It is true for nine, and for three of them that structure did in fact pay zero at least once: Zell's identical instrument paid $39bn at Equity Office and nothing at Tribune; Sawiris was expropriated in Algeria and wiped out in Greece; most of Naval's angel positions are worthless.</p>
<p><strong>Hormozi inverts it.</strong> His decisive move was to <em>stop</em> holding a claim on gym profits and start charging gym owners a price — $6,000 to $16,000 up front, plus a monthly licence. He got rich selling a fee, through a company he happened to own entirely. Calling that "equity income" is a bookkeeping choice, not a fact, and the moment you allow it, the claim can never be wrong about anyone.</p>
<p>So it is printed here as a strong tendency with a named counterexample, rather than as a law. <em>That is the difference between this section and a list of traits.</em></p>
</div>
<div class="note warn">
<span class="lbl">And one that scored ten out of ten and is worthless</span>
<p>"Every one of them had a floor underneath them — a family, a salary, savings, a credential, or a country." It passes all ten. It is also a five-way disjunction ending in <em>or a country</em>, which nothing short of statelessness can fail. It passes roughly 99.9% of humanity, including everyone who has ever gone bankrupt.</p>
<p>It feels profound because the reader silently substitutes the strongest version (Arnault's family fortune, Buffett's Congressman father) and generalises it across all ten. <strong>Watch for that substitution. It is how most wealth writing works.</strong></p>
<p>One narrower version does survive and is worth having: <em>in nine of the ten, the fallback was acquired deliberately BEFORE the concentrated bet — not inherited at birth and not improvised afterwards.</em> Only four were handed a floor. Five built one on purpose. Rihanna is the exception: she bet at sixteen, before she had acquired anything at all.</p>
