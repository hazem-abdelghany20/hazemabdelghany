Attention has four familiar ways of becoming disordered: boredom, envy, resentment, and fear. <button class="ref" data-ref="csikszentmihalyi">Csikszentmihalyi</button> gathered states like these under *psychic entropy*: something inside consciousness pulling against what you chose to do. I will call these four the horsemen because each arrives by a different route and therefore needs a different response. “Stay positive” is not enough. The problem is not a negative mood; it is a specific mechanism taking attention away from you.

**Boredom** is the quietest and often the first door to escape. But boredom does not always mean the task itself is bad. Diagnose it on two axes: **how complex the task is, and how interested you are in it.**

| | Low interest | High interest |
|---|---|---|
| **Low complexity** | Maximum boredom: time-box it, raise the quality bar, batch it, or automate it | Pleasant and easy: useful for rest; add a harder target if the goal is growth |
| **High complexity** | Usually resistance or confusion, not boredom: break it down, learn the missing piece, shorten the feedback loop | The best territory for deep work and flow: a real challenge you genuinely want to solve |

So do not simply make the task harder. If it is easy and dull, add precision, speed, or a higher standard. If it is difficult and uninteresting, adding difficulty will create more escape; break it down, systemize it, or ask whether it deserves doing at all. The next step should be demanding enough to recruit attention but clear and small enough to begin. The same task can feel dull, overwhelming, or absorbing depending on where it sits in this matrix.

**Envy** you already met on the dashboard. It begins with a fast comparison: he arrived and I did not. Leave that sentence vague and it becomes either an attack on him or a verdict that you are inferior. Slow it down and envy changes from general pain into specific information.

Ask three questions. **What exactly do I want?** Not “his life”, perhaps the freedom, craft, money, relationship, or standing. **Do I want the thing itself, or only its public appearance?** Then: **am I willing to pay the price that is missing from the picture?** If yes, choose one repeatable action for this week. If no, stop watching the scoreboard and close the comparison. Envy is not meant to power you forever. It should deliver its information and then leave the decision.

**Resentment** is the heaviest, because it masquerades as justice. Someone took something, credit, money, loyalty, years, and the account is open, and your mind returns to it the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Understand the arithmetic: every replay costs *you* attention today, and costs the other party nothing. Resentment is a tax you levy on yourself and mail to no one. The counter is closing the ledger *unilaterally*, which is what forgiveness actually is, once you strip the sentiment off it. Not declaring the wrong right. Not resuming trust; trust is a separate decision (Part III will handle people). Simply declaring the account closed because the collection effort costs more than the debt. The tradition puts it at the very center, the servant who pardons is raised, the one swallowing rage is beloved, and I don't believe that's primarily for the offender's benefit. Allah is freeing *your* attention. The knight who rides into today's battle still fencing with last year's enemy is fighting two wars with one army, and will lose both.

**Fear**, of the launch, the call, the diagnosis, the empty room where the applause was supposed to be, steals attention by *simulation*: the mind runs the catastrophe on loop, burning today's currency on a tomorrow that mostly never arrives. Two counters, in order. First, interrogate the gauge (Chapter 5): what exactly is exposed, and is the threat real or constructed on a bad night's sleep? Most fear shrinks under a specific question because vagueness was supplying part of its size. Second, for the fear that survives specificity, the real kind, the counter is *contact*. Make the smallest possible contact with the feared thing today: send the first message, book the appointment, write the ugly first page. Action replaces simulation with evidence.

Notice the common structure. All four states take more room when attention is idle and scattered. The organized day, deep blocks, and a fed and rested body do not prevent them from appearing; they reduce how much territory they can occupy. Diagnose boredom with the matrix. Turn envy into information and a decision. Close resentment's account. Meet fear with one real step. Not one slogan for four problems, one precise response for each.
