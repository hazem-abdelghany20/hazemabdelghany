Order in consciousness has four classic enemies, and by now you have the equipment to fight them properly. <button class="ref" data-ref="csikszentmihalyi">Csikszentmihalyi</button>'s list of psychic entropy — worry, envy, resentment, boredom, fear — I've regrouped into the four that actually ride against you daily. Each is a specific *mechanism* of attention-theft, each has a specific counter, and none of them can be beaten by the generic advice to "stay positive." You don't fight cavalry with a slogan.

**Boredom** is the quietest and the most expensive, because it opens the gate for the others. Boredom is not a property of the task — no task is boring in itself; the surgeon's four-hour operation and the gamer's four-hour raid are objectively repetitive. Boredom is attention refusing to engage the present at its actual depth. The counter is not stimulation — stimulation is the trap; it's how the horse gets fed and the baseline gets wrecked. The counter is *depth on purpose*: raise the challenge until it bites. Do the boring task faster, cleaner, to a higher standard; find the one interesting problem inside it; turn the drill into a game with a score. The knight polishing armor for the thousandth time either dies of tedium or discovers that tonight the metal can be brought further than it has ever been. Same task. The difference is a decision about attention.

**Envy** you already met on the instrument panel, so use it as designed: read, then convert. Raw envy is entropy — hours of someone else's highlight reel churning in your chest, attention hemorrhaging into a life that isn't yours. Read envy is *data*: it fires on precisely what you want. So run the protocol every time it burns. Name the specific thing — not "his life," but the thing: the freedom, the craft, the wife, the standing. Ask whether you want it enough to pay its actual invisible price. If yes, extract one action and put it in tomorrow's three — envy converted to fuel is the cheapest fuel there is. If no — and this is shockingly common once the price is named honestly — the envy dissolves on the spot, because it was never desire; it was just the scoreboard glinting. Either way the horseman is off your field by nightfall.

**Resentment** is the heaviest, because it masquerades as justice. Someone took something — credit, money, loyalty, years — and the account is open, and your mind returns to it the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Understand the arithmetic: every replay costs *you* attention today, and costs the other party nothing. Resentment is a tax you levy on yourself and mail to no one. The counter is closing the ledger *unilaterally* — which is what forgiveness actually is, once you strip the sentiment off it. Not declaring the wrong right. Not resuming trust; trust is a separate decision (Part III will handle people). Simply declaring the account closed because the collection effort costs more than the debt. The tradition puts it at the very center — the servant who pardons is raised, the one swallowing rage is beloved — and I don't believe that's primarily for the offender's benefit. Allah is freeing *your* attention. The knight who rides into today's battle still fencing with last year's enemy is fighting two wars with one army, and will lose both.

**Fear** — of the launch, the call, the diagnosis, the empty room where the applause was supposed to be — steals attention by *simulation*: the mind runs the catastrophe on loop, burning today's currency on a tomorrow that mostly never arrives. Two counters, in order. First, interrogate the gauge (Chapter 4): what exactly is exposed, and is the threat real or constructed on a bad night's sleep? Most fear dissolves under a single specific question, because it survives only as a fog. Second, for the fear that survives specificity — the real kind — the counter is *contact*. Fear metabolizes through action and through nothing else; no amount of reassurance, preparation, or waiting has ever ended a real fear. Make the smallest possible contact with the feared thing today: send the first message, book the appointment, write the ugly first page. The simulation cannot survive contact with the actual. It never can. That's the great secret about this horseman: he is a projection on the fog, terrifying at a distance, and made of nothing when touched.

Notice the common structure. All four horsemen are attention parasites, and all four feed on the same host: *idle, ungathered attention*. The trained day of Chapter 8, the deep blocks of Chapter 7, the fed and rested machine of Chapter 1 — these were already the fortification. A mind fully employed on chosen ground offers the horsemen nothing to eat. They ride against every man, every day. They dismount only where the yard is already busy.
