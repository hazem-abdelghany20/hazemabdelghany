This knight came back — that's the first thing to understand. Came back from campaigns, kept coming back, was the reliable one, the strong one, the one others leaned on. And then one season, quietly, something in him stopped. Not the body: the *want*. He looked at the sword and felt nothing. Looked at the hill he'd given nine years and felt less than nothing. He couldn't name the day it happened because there wasn't one — the will hadn't snapped; it had *leaked*, through a hairline crack, for years, while he kept riding because riding was who he was.

Burnout is the modern name, and the modern mistake is treating it as a scheduling problem — take a holiday, come back Monday. But a broken will is not a tired will. Tiredness responds to rest; this responds to nothing, and that unresponsiveness is the signature. So let me do what this book does and look at the mechanics, because the leak has a physics. It is almost never the work itself — humans are built for enormous load; the yard proves it daily. The leak is load *without the systems this book spent four parts building*: effort without the closed ledger (work that's never counted done, Chapter 8's close skipped for years); output-grading of daily effort (every market wobble billed to the self, Chapter 14's clocks crossed until the self is bankrupt); losses never mourned (Chapter 18's invoices stuffed unread in a drawer, compounding); a starved machine (Chapter 1 — sleep, sun, training — mortgaged season after season for "one more push"); and no solitude, ever, so the filing department never once ran (Chapter 5), and twenty years of undigested campaigning just sat there, fermenting. Any one of these leaks slowly. All five together is how the strong ones — specifically the strong ones, because only they can run broken this long — end up in this room.

I have not lived this room, but I have stood in its doorway, and I know the direction of it. My June: the surgery took the body, the systems fell like dominoes, and a few weeks in I logged a week of *zero out of seven* on habits I had kept for a year — and what frightened me wasn't the zero; it was how *reasonable* the zero felt, how the not-wanting had its own calm voice. The way back, for me, was not motivation — nothing in that month responded to motivation. It was one anchor rebuilt (the phone rule, then the prayers, then one block), plus something I had to swallow pride to use: I asked to be held accountable, out loud, by name. Told the people around me to call out the relapse, not narrate it politely. Chapter 8 said accountability is architecture, not weakness. In the doorway of this room, it is the *handrail*.

Which is the real content of this chapter: **this room, alone among the five, is mostly preventable — and barely curable.** Before the break, everything works: the gauges of Chapter 4 (the flatness, the cynicism, the dread of things you used to love — those are readings, not moods; believe them early), the maintenance of the machine, the mourning done on time, the ledger closed nightly, the handrail installed before it's needed. After the break, almost nothing works quickly, and the return is measured in seasons — a long, flat convalescence of tiny anchors, honest company, and a mercy the broken man must extend to himself daily, which is precisely the mercy this book's harsh-ledger voice must learn to speak here: the effort ledger of a broken man is not empty. *Bearing the brokenness is the effort now.* Sabr was never only for external weather. Sometimes the load to be carried well is you.

And for the reader in the doorway right now — not broken, leaking: you already know. That's why this page feels like a mirror. Close the book. Rebuild one anchor tonight. Tell one person the true sentence. The room behind this door has no floor, and you are nearer the hinge than you're telling yourself. Go.
