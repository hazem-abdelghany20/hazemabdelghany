The last skill of the drill is the strangest one to modern ears: deliberately giving up options. Every knight lived under a code — vows, obligations, lines that could not be crossed regardless of advantage. Our century reads that as a cage and measures freedom in options kept open. The knight would have laughed at us, and he would have been right, because he knew something we've forgotten: **a man with no code doesn't make free choices. He makes ten thousand exhausted ones.**

Look at the mechanics. Every open option is a standing negotiation, and every negotiation is billed in the two currencies you've spent this whole book learning to guard: attention, and the horse. The man with no rule about his phone renegotiates with the feed forty times a day — and the feed only has to win once an hour to own him. The man with the rule — *phone in the other room during blocks* — negotiates zero times. It's simply the shape of his life, the way the wall is the shape of the house. A rule is a decision made once, by the best version of you, and then enforced automatically on all the lesser versions who show up at 11 p.m., tired and negotiable. That's not a cage. That's the strong you protecting the weak you — which is the only protection racket that has ever been worth the price.

This is why constraint produces freedom rather than costing it, and you already own the proof. The five prayers look like the ultimate constraint — fixed times, no negotiation, your day interrupted five times regardless of what you're building. Live them properly and you discover they're the *architecture*: pitons in the day's rock face, rhythm no calendar app has ever matched, five guaranteed exits from the churn before it can compound. And Ramadan is the masterclass — the hungriest month is somehow the clearest, the discipline spilling sideways into everything, and the secret is not caloric: for one month the entire day has a code, and ten thousand small negotiations simply vanish. Everyone who has lived a real Ramadan has felt the paradox: never more constrained, never more free. The cage was the scaffolding.

So write yours. Three rules for building a code that holds:

**Few, and non-negotiable — never many and flexible.** Every rule spends enforcement budget, and a code of twenty rules collapses in a fortnight, taking your self-trust with it. Five rules kept are worth fifty intended. Mine are single-digit and boring: phone out of the room during deep work. The hardest block before the first scroll. No new commitment on the day I hear the pitch (the horse loves a pitch; the rule makes it sleep on it). Training and prayers are appointments, not intentions — they get missed only the way a meeting with a king gets missed. And in the business: rules I wrote for my ad accounts precisely because I knew the 11 p.m. version of me would panic — never kill a campaign in its first 48 hours, never scale on a number I haven't verified at the source. A code isn't only for the soul. It's for anywhere a weaker future you will hold the pen.

**Write them when calm, obey them when not.** The whole value of a code is that it was authored by you-at-your-best with full information and no pressure. Which yields the operating rule: *never renegotiate in the moment.* The moment is precisely when you are least qualified — tired, tempted, flattered, afraid. If a rule genuinely needs changing, change it next week, in writing, in daylight. A rule you'll rewrite at midnight under fire was never a rule; it was a mood wearing a uniform.

**And when you break one — repair, never spiral.** You will break them; you are not a machine, and the code is not a verdict on your worth. The danger isn't the break — it's the second thing: the *in for a penny* collapse, where one missed block becomes a lost day becomes a lost week, because the broken rule "proves" you were never the kind of man who keeps one. That proof is a lie of the horsemen. The code's real test was never perfection. It is the speed of return. Miss the prayer, pray the next one on time. Lose the morning, close the day properly anyway. The knight who falls in the yard and stands is training. The knight who falls and lies there, narrating his fall as identity — he's the only one actually failing.

The drill is complete: the blade held steady, the day made a unit, the horsemen met, the forge lit, the code written. Everything so far has been inside your walls — your hour, your day, your equipment, your rules. High ground, high control.

Now we ride out of the yard. And the first question outside the gate is the one flow couldn't answer, the one all this magnificent capacity has been silently waiting on: *which war?*

---

*A campaign is where the knight's control begins to leak. In the yard, everything answered to him. On campaign, he chooses the war, the road, and the company he rides with — and then the weather votes, the terrain votes, allies vote, and the enemy votes. His choices still matter enormously. They are just no longer the only thing that matters.*

*This part is about the choices that play out over seasons and years: what to aim at, how long to stay, how to keep score honestly, and what to do about the fact that other people are free. Control here is partial — which is uncomfortable, and exactly why most people either grip harder or let go entirely. Both are mistakes. The skill of the campaign is holding the reins loosely and riding anyway.*
