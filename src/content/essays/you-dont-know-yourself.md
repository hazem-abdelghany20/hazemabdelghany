Play back a recording of your own voice and you'll say the same thing everyone says: *that's not me.*

It is. The reason it sounds wrong is mechanical. When you speak, you hear yourself partly through the bones of your own skull, which deepens and warms the sound. Everyone else hears only the version that travels through the air. So the voice every person in your life knows is the one voice you have never actually heard.

That's the whole essay in one fact. **The version of you that everyone else deals with is the version you have the least access to.**

## You don't even know your body

Start with the easy part — the part you can see.

You don't know how you look when you walk. You don't know how you look when you sit. Close your eyes and try to turn your own face around in your head, like a 3D model, and it won't hold still. When someone tells you *so-and-so looks just like you*, you nod politely, because you genuinely can't see it. Everyone else can.

The speaker I heard this from cited a study where people photographed their hands, the photos were shuffled, and a meaningful share of them could not pick out their own. Whatever the exact number, the point survives: there are parts of your own body you have never seen and never touched.

That's the body. The thing you look at every day, in mirrors, in photos. Now ask how well you know what you *can't* photograph — your temperament, your faults, what you do under pressure.

## The proof is surprise

If you knew yourself, you would never be surprised by what people think of you. And you are, constantly. *People say you're like this.* — *Me?* And then, a year later, alone: *…yeah. I am like that.*

You're certain you'll succeed at something, and you fail. You're certain you're not built for something, and it turns out to be the thing you're best at. Anyone who has built anything learns this in the most expensive possible way.

There's a verse I had read many times without noticing what it was doing. Describing how well the People of the Book recognised the Prophet ﷺ, the Qur'an says: *they know him as they know their sons.* Most people, if you ask them to finish the verse from memory, will say *as they know themselves.* But it doesn't say that. It says **sons**.

Al-Raghib al-Isfahani explained why: a person doesn't know himself until a good stretch of his life has passed. Knowing your son is immediate. Knowing yourself comes later — after you've fallen on your head, been let down, failed, hurt. *Then* you start saying *I've matured, I understand now.* Your standards rise: in who you choose as friends, which projects you take on, what upsets you and what doesn't, which fights you enter and which you let pass.

Self-knowledge is not a starting point. It is earned, and it is usually expensive.

## Your phone cut you off from yourself

Here is something that has happened to all of us, and to some of us daily. You're sitting with the phone, scrolling, for a long time. You put it down, get up — and your hand hurts. Your neck hurts.

Did the pain start when you stood up? No. It was there the whole time. You just weren't there to feel it. All of your awareness was in the screen, and the moment you left the screen, your body finally got to report in.

That's a **physical** pain — loud, direct, with nerves built to deliver it. Now think about everything in your life that doesn't hurt on schedule: the goal you keep postponing, the relationship you've let go quiet, the responsibility you're letting slide, the fault that repeats. None of those send a signal sharp enough to break through a feed.

Social media first cut us off from each other — a room full of people, each on their own screen. Then it cut us off from ourselves. I track my screen time every week. The number tells me how long I was gone. It can't tell me what I missed while I was gone.

## The visible part and the hidden part

Ibn al-Muqaffa wrote that a person's worst fault is that his faults are hidden from him. The older saying is sharper: *one of you sees the speck in his brother's eye, and forgets the log in his own.* I can spot my colleague's mistakes, my team's mistakes, from across the room. My own, standing inside them, I can't see.

You can't ask a fish what water looks like. Closeness is its own kind of veil.

Think of the self like a garment you're wearing. Part of it is in view. If you tell me there's a stain on my sleeve, I look down, check, and tell you whether you're right — your comment doesn't shake me, because I can see for myself. That's the visible part. I know it, I can control it, I can clean it.

The problem is the back. The part I can't turn my head far enough to see. If you tell me there's something on my back, I'm stuck. I can't verify it. I either dismiss you or I go looking for help.

## Where to start

A few honest tools:

**Account for yourself.** A few minutes each night: what did I do today, and why? *Muḥāsaba* — the old practice of auditing the self — is still the cheapest instrument there is.

**Go into new experiences.** You learn more about yourself in one unfamiliar situation than in a year of routine, because routine never asks you anything new.

**Tests help, a little.** Personality tools can hand you a vocabulary. They can't hand you your back.

And for the back, there is only one real tool: someone who can see it and will tell you. That's the subject of [The Mirror](/essays/the-mirror/).

> Not knowing yourself isn't the problem. Being sure that you do is.

This is the first of four stages of change — know, admit, decide, repeat — laid out in [Tazkiya](/essays/tazkiya/). Every other stage depends on it. You cannot admit what you cannot see.
