Here is the failure mode that all of Part I and II cannot save you from: **the wrong war, fought excellently.** A man with governed attention, a trained body, a deep-work practice, and an iron code — pointed at a hill that was never worth taking. He will climb it faster than anyone. That is the tragedy. Skill determines how well you climb. Direction determines whether the climb was worth a decade of your one life. And direction is decided in a handful of quiet moments that almost nobody treats with the seriousness they deserve — usually less deliberately than people choose a phone.

So how do you choose a hill? Three tests, and be suspicious of any hill that passes fewer than all three.

**Can you become genuinely great at it?** Not "do you love it today" — the follow-your-passion doctrine has ruined more twenty-somethings than laziness has, because passion follows competence far more often than it precedes it. <button class="ref" data-ref="newport">Cal Newport</button>'s correction is the right one: ask instead where you can accumulate rare and valuable skill — *career capital* — fastest. That is partly aptitude, but mostly it is fit between the work and your appetite for its specific pains. Every craft has a tax: sales taxes rejection, engineering taxes tedium, media taxes exposure, my world of ads taxes daily public failure in numbers. You will only stay long enough to get great at a thing whose tax you can pay without resenting it. Find the pain you're strangely fine with. That's not a poetic line; it is the most reliable career predictor I know.

**Does the hill pay — and does the terrain rise?** Effort compounds only where the ground slopes upward. The identical decade of excellent work yields wildly different lives in a growing market versus a dying one, and this is decided before you start, by choice, not effort. This is where imagination earns its keep as a campaign tool: run the film forward honestly. Not "do I like this work" but "what does year seven of this look like — who is paying, for what, and is that pool growing?" The knight who mastered siegecraft as gunpowder arrived was not less disciplined than his grandfather. He chose a hill history was already walking away from.

**Can you stand on it for years without becoming someone you despise?** Every hill shapes its climber. Spend a decade in a field and you will absorb its values, keep its company, adopt its definition of winning — the hill is training you the whole time you are climbing it (trainability never sleeps, and it doesn't check whether the change is one you'd have chosen). Look hard at the people fifteen years ahead of you on the path. Not the outliers — the *typical* ones. That is the current pulling on you. If you don't want to become them, no salary rewrites that math.

Two disciplines once you've chosen. **First, count the cost of the unchosen hills — then actually drop them.** I know this one from the inside: at one point I was seriously running eight brands. Eight. It felt like ambition; it was actually a refusal to choose, dressed up as energy — because every kept option bills you monthly in attention (Chapter 2), and eight small climbs sum to zero summits. The portfolio only started compounding when I forced the honest sort: two ventures that get my real hours, and the rest demoted — out loud, in writing — to hobbies. The demotion stung for a week. The focus has paid for a year. A campaign is defined at least as much by the wars you decline as the one you ride to.

**Second, write the kill criteria before you fall in love.** Choosing a hill while standing at its base, you are the most objective you will ever be about it. A year in — sweat sunk, identity attached, people watching — you will be the least. So decide *now* what evidence would mean this hill is wrong: which number, missed for how long; which door, closed how many times. Write it down and date it. This is not pessimism. It is leaving a letter from the clear-eyed general to the sunk-cost soldier you will become. Part IV will have much more to say about reading results — but the letter must be written here, at the base, while your eyes still work.

And hold all of it loosely, because here is the truth of this part: choosing the hill is a judgment made in fog, with partial information, about a future that will not consult you. You can choose *well* — that is a skill, and everything above trains it. You cannot choose *correctly* on demand; correctness is only visible from the far side, and sometimes the best-chosen hill still turns out barren. That thought is not a reason to choose casually. It is the first rumble of the weather this book has been promising you — and the whole subject of the part after this one.

Choose deliberately. Ride committed. Hold loosely. All three at once — that is the posture of the campaign.
