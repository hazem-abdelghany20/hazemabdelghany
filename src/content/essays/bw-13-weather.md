<h3>What the rows actually say</h3>
<div class="rule-list">
<div class="item"><h4><span class="n">—</span>Nobody burned the boats</h4><p><strong>Zero out of ten.</strong> The single most repeated piece of advice in this genre is the one thing not one person in this book did. Blakely sold fax machines door to door through the entire early build and resigned two weeks before the Oprah segment aired. Buffett ran his own money while working for Graham. Bezos left Wall Street only after banking the pay and with his parents' cheque behind him. "Go all in" is advice given by survivors to people who cannot afford to take it.</p></div>
<div class="item"><h4><span class="n">—</span>You do not need investors</h4><p>Hormozi took zero outside capital, ever. Blakely took zero and owned 100% for twenty-three years, which is the entire reason her exit was worth what it was. Both are among the fastest and cleanest fortunes here. Seven of ten did raise — raising is a choice with a price, not a requirement.</p></div>
<div class="item"><h4><span class="n">—</span>You do not need an original idea</h4><p>Not one of the ten invented anything. Cut-off pantyhose. A store. Other people's century-old names. A turnaround someone had already run. If "I do not have an original idea" is what is stopping you, it stopped none of them.</p></div>
<div class="item"><h4><span class="n">—</span>You do not need a hot category</h4><p>Blakely entered <em>hosiery</em>, a structurally declining category, and won on price and shelf placement. Arnault bought European names that were commercially dead. Buffett's vehicle was a failing textile mill. Sawiris deliberately took the markets global operators had refused. The requirement is a <em>reachable</em> market, not a fashionable one.</p></div>
<div class="item"><h4><span class="n">—</span>You do not need a patent or a moat to start</h4><p>Spanx was copied within months and won anyway, on distribution and brand; Blakely wrote her own patent from a textbook and it was effectively decorative. Only Nvidia has anything resembling defensible technical IP, and that moat was a software ecosystem built over a decade, not a filing.</p></div>
<div class="item"><h4><span class="n">—</span>You do not need decades</h4><p>Only four of ten took longer than fifteen years. Bezos reached a billion in four. Rihanna's beauty company did it in four. Hormozi did it in five. What none of them skipped was the runway. <strong>The engine can be fast. The preparation never was.</strong></p></div>
<div class="item"><h4><span class="n">—</span>You do not need technical skill</h4><p>Seven of ten had none relevant to their fortune. Arnault cannot design a handbag. Blakely could not sew. Zell never built a building. If you are technical that is a genuine edge — and it is not the price of entry.</p></div>
<div class="item"><h4><span class="n">—</span>You do not need an elite education or inherited money</h4><p>Rihanna left school at sixteen. Blakely went to a state university and failed the law-school entrance exam. Four of the ten started at or below zero. Be symmetric about it: four others started with a great deal, and Arnault's real advantage was not his family's money but his family's <em>credit</em> — he raised four times their entire net worth on the strength of who they were.</p></div>
<div class="item"><h4><span class="n">—</span>You do not need to suffer publicly</h4><p>The story we least like to give up. Blakely was never broke, never in debt, and profitable in year one. Two of ten had the long public humiliation. <strong>Hardship is not a qualification. It is an occupational hazard most people who experience it never recover from</strong> — the survivors are visible and the rest are not, which is exactly why the story feels true.</p></div>
<div class="item"><h4><span class="n">—</span>You do not need an exit — or to never sell</h4><p>Neither slogan survives. Five of ten never sold a share. Four needed the sale, and for Blakely it was her first real liquidity in twenty-three years. What survives is narrower: somebody credible has to price it eventually.</p></div>
<div class="item"><h4><span class="n">—</span>You do not need an audience</h4><p>Two of ten. One built it over twelve years on somebody else's capital; the other's arrived <em>after</em> the money and because of it. Buffett was anonymous outside Omaha for twenty-five years. <strong>"Build an audience, then monetise it" describes two of these ten and is sold to beginners as though it described all of them.</strong></p></div>
<div class="item"><h4><span class="n">—</span>You do not have to be Western — but where the asset is <em>titled</em> matters enormously</h4><p>Two of the ten are not Western, and the Western-institutions pattern is largely an artefact of who ends up in books like this. One finding does survive, and it is the sharpest here. <strong>Where you live matters far less than where your claim is registered and who can revoke it.</strong> Sawiris's most profitable business was a licence granted by the Algerian state — and taken back by the Algerian state the moment it became worth taking. He spent eight years in international arbitration and recovered nothing. Blakely's patent and trademark were enforceable against companies vastly richer than her, which is how a woman with $5,000 and a day job held a national retailer to terms. <em>If the party that gave you the thing can take it back unilaterally, you do not own it.</em></p></div>
</div>
<div class="note ochre">
<span class="lbl">The one worth sitting with</span>
<p>Nine of these ten were funded for years by exactly the thing wealth books tell you to escape — a salary, a fee, a commission, royalties, carry, or family money. Blakely on commission. Buffett's first thirteen years on a performance fee charged on other people's money. Bezos on a Wall Street salary. Huang on someone else's payroll for a decade. Rihanna on touring and endorsement fees for twelve years before Fenty existed.</p>
<p><strong>Your income is the funding mechanism for the stake, not its enemy.</strong></p>
