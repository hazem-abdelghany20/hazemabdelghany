<p>The most damaging thing about reading one wealth book is that it sounds settled. Read five and you discover the field is not a body of knowledge but an argument, and that the people at the top of it flatly contradict each other on load-bearing questions. That is not a weakness in the field — it is the most useful thing about it, because each contradiction has a <em>condition</em> attached, and finding your condition is how you pick a road.</p>
<h3>One · Raise the price, or cut it?</h3>
<div class="clash">
<div class="side"><span class="who">Arnault</span><p>Restrict supply and raise price. Never discount, never run an outlet, own every shop it is sold in. Margin is the business, and scarcity is what protects it.</p></div>
<div class="vs">versus</div>
<div class="side"><span class="who">Bezos</span><p>Cut price relentlessly and flood supply. Give the margin away to buy volume, then own the layer everyone must pass through. Scale is the business, and margin is the bait.</p></div>
</div>
<p class="resolve"><strong>Both built giants, so price is not the variable — the moat is.</strong> If what you sell derives value from <em>meaning</em> (who owns it, what it says, how hard it is to get), scarcity is your protection and discounting destroys it permanently. If it derives value from <em>function</em>, scale economics protect you and any margin you hold onto is an invitation to a competitor. The fatal position is the middle: a functional product priced as though it were a meaningful one. <em>Pick a side deliberately, then behave consistently for years.</em></p>
<h3>Two · Never sell, or sell well?</h3>
<div class="clash">
<div class="side"><span class="who">Zell</span><p>Selling <em>is</em> the skill. "Any time you don't sell, you buy." Reprice your holdings continuously and sell when the market will pay more than the asset is worth to you.</p></div>
<div class="vs">versus</div>
<div class="side"><span class="who">Buffett &amp; Huang</span><p>Never sell. One dividend in sixty years; thirty-three years without a liquidity event. Selling interrupts the compounding and triggers the tax, and the exponent is where the money is.</p></div>
</div>
<p class="resolve"><strong>The split is clean, and it turns on one question: does your asset compound on its own, or does it need a cycle?</strong> Buffett's businesses generate cash and reinvest it whether he acts or not, so time is his ally and selling is pure loss. Zell's buildings do not compound — they ride credit cycles — so time is his enemy and exiting is the entire craft. <em>Ask honestly which kind you own.</em> And note the third case: Rihanna owns a compounding-looking asset that is actually depreciating, held it like Buffett, and is watching the mark fall.</p>
<h3>Three · Which leverage is the good one?</h3>
<div class="clash">
<div class="side"><span class="who">Naval</span><p>Code and media. They replicate at zero cost and need nobody's permission, which is why a person with a laptop can start today and a person waiting for capital cannot.</p></div>
<div class="vs">versus</div>
<div class="side"><span class="who">Buffett</span><p>Capital — and the strongest evidence ever assembled is that permissioned capital, obtained cheaply enough, beats everything. Float at negative cost is the most powerful leverage that has ever existed.</p></div>
</div>
<p class="resolve"><strong>They are both right and the disagreement is about starting position, not about physics.</strong> Capital is the higher-return leverage and it is closed to you at zero. Code and media are lower-return and genuinely open this afternoon. The practical reading is sequential rather than competitive: <em>permissionless leverage is how you get to the point where permissioned leverage becomes available to you.</em> Four of the ten in this book made their fortune on capital — and every one of them had access to it before they started.</p>
<h3>Four · Conviction, or feedback?</h3>
<div class="clash">
<div class="side"><span class="who">Thiel</span><p>Definite optimism. Have a specific plan and hold it with conviction. Iteration, testing and incrementalism are what people do when they have no secret and no vision.</p></div>
<div class="vs">versus</div>
<div class="side"><span class="who">Taleb</span><p>In fat-tailed domains you cannot forecast, so never take a position that cannot survive being wrong. Many small bounded bets, no single unbounded one.</p></div>
</div>
<p class="resolve"><strong>The evidence is unkind to conviction.</strong> Thiel's own hedge fund ran the exact posture his book prescribes — one high-conviction thesis, no feedback loop — and collapsed. Meanwhile Huang, the great conviction story, quietly funded his decade-long bet out of a boring profitable business rather than betting the company. <em>The synthesis is: be definite about direction and bounded about exposure.</em> Conviction is free; irreversibility is what costs you. If you cannot survive being wrong for three more years, you are not being convicted — you are being reckless, and the two feel identical from the inside.</p>
<h3>Five · One thing, or many?</h3>
<div class="clash">
<div class="side"><span class="who">Hormozi &amp; Huang</span><p>One thing, past the point where it is boring. Thirty-three years in one company. One offer, one price, one script, sold thousands of times identically. Variance is what kills.</p></div>
<div class="vs">versus</div>
<div class="side"><span class="who">Sawiris &amp; Arnault</span><p>Many at once. Mobile networks in five countries simultaneously. Dozens of brands acquired across four decades. Breadth is the strategy.</p></div>
</div>
<p class="resolve"><strong>This one only looks like a contradiction, and seeing through it is worth more than any other page here.</strong> Sawiris was not running five businesses — he was running <em>one playbook in five geographies</em>: buy the licence nobody will price, launch fast, own prepaid distribution. Arnault has performed the identical act perhaps thirty times: buy a heritage name, plug it into owned retail, raise price. Neither man diversified his <em>method</em>. They replicated one method across many instances.</p>
<div class="note warn"><p><strong>So the real rule is: one playbook, as many instances as you can run.</strong> The trap that catches capable people is the opposite — many playbooks, one instance each. Five businesses that each require a different skill, a different buyer and a different form of leverage is not a portfolio. It is five apprenticeships running concurrently, none of which will ever reach mastery, and it is the most respectable-looking way to stay unwealthy.</p></div>
