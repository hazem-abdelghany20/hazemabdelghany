Every knight priced the enemy into his plans. The arrow from the facing line, the siege, the honest hatred of the man across the field — all of it expected, trained for, almost respectable. What no training covers is the arrow from behind: the brother-in-arms who sold the gate. The lord who spent his loyalty like copper. The friend at court whose testimony, it turns out, had a price. Betrayal is not a bigger wound than battle. It is a different *kind* — because it doesn't just take the thing; it reaches backward through time and poisons the memory of every shared year, and it doesn't just wound the man; it wounds his *instrument for trusting*, which he needs to live.

Let the book's frame hold this steady, because the frame is the way through. Another person's choice is the purest output there is — Chapter 15 built the whole doctrine: free will that is not yours, zero control in the one dimension that matters. You could not have controlled it. Could you have *predicted* it? Sometimes — and Chapter 14's honest audit applies here too, without mercy and without masochism: were there readings you refused to take? Gauges (Chapter 4) whose reports you overrode because the partnership was load-bearing and you needed it to be fine? Take that tuition if it's owed; it's expensive and non-refundable, so at least collect the lesson. But when the audit is done, most real betrayal remains what it is: a free choice, made in another chest, for reasons that live there. **His choice is his ledger.** The tradition is precise about this — every soul earns against itself alone, and no bearer of burdens carries another's. The betrayal sits, whole and permanent, in *his* account. The only thing that can move it into yours is what you do next.

Because here is the actual danger of this room, and it is not the loss — the loss is survivable; you've done Chapter 18, you know how losses close. The danger is the *conversion*: betrayed men have a way of taking the knife out of their back and spending the rest of their lives holding it toward everyone else. Never trusting again feels like wisdom, costs like wisdom never does — because trust was never optional (Chapter 15: nothing worth building gets built solo), so the man who armors completely doesn't become safe; he becomes *alone*, which is just betrayal's work, finished by its victim, for free, forever. One man's one choice, amortized over every future relationship. That is the only version of this story where the traitor truly wins.

So the way out has three moves, in order. **Collect the lesson, not the worldview** — the audit's tuition, taken precisely: *this* structure was fragile, *these* readings were overridden; never the global rewrite "people are treacherous," which is bad statistics (one data point, Chapter 17) hardened into a life sentence you serve yourself. **Close the account unilaterally** — Chapter 9's forgiveness, now at its full weight: not declaring it right, not resuming trust (trust is rebuilt by evidence or not at all; forgiveness is free, re-entry is earned), just refusing to fund the replay with one more day of attention. The rage rehearsals, the imaginary confrontations, the case you keep perfecting for a court that will never convene — that's your armory, hemorrhaging into his account. He took years. The replay offers him the rest. Decline. **Then trust again, deliberately, with better architecture** — structures that survive a walk-out, paper where paper belongs, gauges kept on, and the heart nonetheless genuinely open — because the resilient structure is what makes open-heartedness affordable (Chapter 15's whole doctrine), and because the alternative is his knife, held forever, in your hand.

The knight who was betrayed and rode out again with company — watchful, structured, unarmored at the heart — is not naive. He has simply refused to let one man's ledger become his cage. Of all the rooms in this part, this is the one where the door out is most clearly marked. It's just that the handle is forgiveness, and everyone in the room keeps saying the handle is justice — and justice, worth pursuing where courts exist, was never what the door runs on. The door runs on your attention, released. Turn the handle. Ride.
