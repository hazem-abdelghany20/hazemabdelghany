Choosing the hill does not shorten the road. It only makes it more likely that the years you spend will be spent somewhere worthwhile. Even after a good choice, the work usually takes years before it becomes distinctive rather than merely competent.

When <button class="ref" data-ref="mastery">Robert Greene</button> studied the lives of people who reached mastery, from Da Vinci to Faraday, he found quiet beginnings: watching skilled practitioners, imitating them, doing small repetitive work, and slowly developing judgment. That period was not a waiting room before the important work. It created the capacity for important work. Thousands of attempts teach the eye to notice distinctions a beginner cannot yet see and allow the hand to act without rebuilding the process from first principles every time.

Greene's advice is powerful, but read too simply it becomes "choose early and never look around." <button class="ref" data-ref="range">David Epstein</button> offers the necessary correction. In *Range*, he distinguishes between kinds of learning environments. Some are stable. Their rules are known and their feedback is fast and accurate. Chess is an easy example: the board stays the same, the rules hold, and mistakes are visible. In environments like these, an early start and narrow practice can create a real advantage.

Other fields are unstable. Rules change, feedback arrives late, and the same decision can succeed in one context and fail in another. Business, strategy, product building, leadership, and creative work are closer to this kind. Here, breadth becomes useful. People who have worked across several domains have more examples to compare, can transfer an idea from one field into another, and are less likely to mistake one familiar method for a universal law.

One of Epstein's most useful ideas is **match quality**: the fit between the person and the work. The question is not only whether you can become good at a craft. It is whether its daily problems suit you, whether your strengths matter there, and whether you can tolerate its particular costs. Reflection alone cannot answer those questions. You have to try things. A person who changes direction early is not necessarily falling behind. Sometimes they are spending a few years to avoid spending a lifetime on a bad fit.

I recognize my own path in that argument. The tutoring project taught me selling and teaching. The clothing brand taught me product, inventory, and customers. Client work and advertising taught me to tie judgment to evidence. Courses, agency experiments, and technology taught me to break a problem apart and build a system around it. None was the whole answer, but each left me with a tool I now use. My current work needs the combination: business, technology, marketing, and people in the same room. Earlier specialization in only one of them might have made me deeper in that lane and weaker at the compound work that fits me better.

This is also why Greene and Epstein apply differently across fields. If the rules are stable and excellence depends on precise execution of a known skill, specialize earlier and take Greene seriously. If the field changes quickly and the work requires ideas from several disciplines, allow a longer sampling period and take Epstein seriously. Once you find strong match quality, both lead to the same demand: stay long enough for experience to compound.

The choice is not between being a specialist or a generalist forever. It is knowing which stage you are in. **Sample enough to choose intelligently, then commit enough to become excellent.**

Sampling still has to produce information. A project, a job, or a false start can be a real search for fit. But opening something new whenever the old thing becomes tedious is not range. It is avoidance. Useful experiments leave answers behind: What did I learn? What suited me? What do I not want to repeat? Which skill came with me into the next attempt?

After commitment, the hardest period is rarely the beginning. The beginning brings novelty and quick gains. The difficulty arrives after novelty disappears and before mastery becomes visible. You may spend years improving while the improvement remains hard for you or anyone else to see. Then the uncomfortable question returns: did I choose badly, or am I simply in the slow part of learning?

No slogan can answer that. Return to the exit conditions you wrote, examine the accumulated evidence, and ask people who understand the work. If the signals still show fit and improvement, do not mistake boredom for an instruction to leave. Continue by structure, because excitement will not carry the full distance.

Years alone are not experience. Experience compounds when it is reviewed and understood. After each project, write down what worked, what failed, and what you misunderstood. Keep the cases, decisions, and outcomes. Patterns gradually emerge. A situation that required a week in year two may become obvious at a glance in year eight because you have seen dozens of its relatives.

Two people can work in the same field for ten years. One has ten years of experience; the other has repeated their first year ten times. The difference is not time or talent. It is whether each cycle changed the next one.

Stay long enough, but stay awake. Mastery needs persistence and attention together.
